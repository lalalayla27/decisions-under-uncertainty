Part 1

Stochastic optimal control

Choosing actions over time, based on what you observe, to optimize an expected objective when the system evolves randomly.

State xtWhere the robot is now
Control utMove ↑ → ↓ ←
Noise wtWith probability p it slides sideways
ObjectiveMaximize E[Σ γt rt]: reward now beats reward later
xt+1 = f(xt, ut, wt)

Idea 1: the answer is a policy, not a plan

A fixed route ignores where the randomness actually took you. A feedback policy u = π(x) re-decides at every step. Test it: “Release 1,000 robots” →

Idea 2: Bellman's principle of optimality (1957)

Whatever happens first, the remaining decisions must be optimal from wherever you land. That gives a recursive equation for the best achievable value V*:

V*(s) = maxa Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) [ r + γ V*(s′) ]

The lavender part is the model: the known probabilities. Dynamic programming solves it by applying this update to every state until the values stop changing (value iteration).

The formal definition (continuous time)

The state follows a stochastic differential equation: drift b plus random diffusion σ dW.

dXt = b(t, Xt, ut) dt + σ(t, Xt, ut) dWt

Choose controls to minimize expected running cost plus terminal cost:

J(u) = E[ ∫0T f(t, Xt, ut) dt + g(XT) ]

Two classic routes: dynamic programming → the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman PDE (our grid is its discrete version), or the stochastic maximum principle (a Hamiltonian plus a backward "adjoint" equation, like shadow prices). Modern variants add robustness to model uncertainty, risk constraints and delays, and are increasingly solved with deep learning.

Beyond the grid:
LQG control: Kalman filter + LQR (the Kalman filter flew on Apollo) Merton (1969): investing & consuming under random returns Inventory: order-up-to policies under random demand Revenue management & dynamic pricing Finance: option pricing, mean–variance portfolios, optimal execution

Part 2

Reinforcement learning

How RL makes the best decisions when the future is uncertain, and how it relates to stochastic optimal control.

Why do we need reinforcement learning?

Many real-world decisions are made sequentially:

🚗 Self-driving carConstantly decides how to steer and brake
🩺 DoctorAdjusts treatment based on the patient's response over time
Decisions affect future outcomesFuture outcomes are uncertainWe need a strategy that works over the long run

What is reinforcement learning?

A machine learning framework in which an agent learns through interaction with an environment.

The agent ① observes its current situation, ② takes an action, ③ receives a reward, and ④ learns which actions lead to better outcomes. Over time, its strategy improves.

“Learning optimal decisions through trial and error to maximize long-term rewards.”

RL as a statistical problem

RL is fundamentally about uncertainty and expectations. Future states, rewards and environmental conditions are all random. With state S and action A, we work with transition probabilities:

P(St+1 | St, At)

The probability of moving to the next state, given the current state and action. On our grid: 80% the intended square, 10% each side.

Objective: maximize expected future reward

max E [ Σt=0∞ γt Rt ]
RtReward received at time t
γDiscount factor (0 ≤ γ < 1): later reward counts less
EExpectation, because the future is random

Not the biggest reward today, but the most total expected reward over the whole future. That long-term view is what makes RL powerful.

Markov decision processes (MDPs)

Most RL problems are modeled as MDPs. The key assumption is the Markov property: the future depends only on the current state (and action), not on the entire past.

P(St+1 | St, At, St−1, At−1, …) = P(St+1 | St, At)
StatesActionsTransition probabilitiesRewards

These four ingredients are the mathematical framework for RL, and the same framework as stochastic control in Part 1.

Value function: how good is a state?

V(s) = E[ future rewards | S = s ] = E[ Σ γt Rt | S0 = s ]

The expected future reward when starting from state s. From a statistics view it's a conditional expectation, and much of RL is about estimating it accurately. The numbers in each grid square are exactly these values.

How the agent learns: Q-learning

Replace the average over P with one experienced transition and nudge the estimate toward it (Watkins, 1989):

Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α [ r + γ maxa′ Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a) ]

The underlined term is the surprise: what happened minus what was expected. Explore vs. exploit: with probability ε the agent tries a random action; otherwise it takes the best one known so far.

Reinforcement learning vs. stochastic optimal control

Both aim to make optimal decisions under uncertainty. The major difference is how much information is available.

Stochastic optimal controlReinforcement learning
AssumesWe know the system dynamics, the probability distributions and the mathematical modelThe model is unknown
How it decidesOptimization computes the best decisionsThe agent learns from experience and observed data
BottleneckComputationData & exploration

RL can be viewed as a data-driven approach to solving stochastic optimal control problems.

Part 3

Implementing them on a computer

Every implementation follows the same pipeline. The choice between planning and learning comes down to what you know about the randomness.

STEP 1

Model it as an MDP

States, actions, rewards, discount γ. Usually the hardest and most important step.

STEP 2a

Know P and it's small?

Dynamic programming: value or policy iteration. Exact answer.

STEP 2b

Only a simulator or data?

RL: Q-learning (tables) or DQN/PPO (neural nets) when there are too many states for a table.

STEP 3

Evaluate vs. a baseline

Monte Carlo rollouts against a simple rule, exactly like the 1,000 robots.

Wrap-up

  
Three things to remember

  

    
1
Stochastic control computes
When you know how the randomness behaves, the Bellman equation gives the optimal feedback policy, and risk can change what's optimal.

    
2
RL learns
Same goal, same equation, but it learns from sampled experience when the model is unknown or too big to write down.

    
3
Pick by what you know
Small and known: DP. Simulator or data only: RL. Huge: approximate with neural networks. Always evaluate against a baseline.
